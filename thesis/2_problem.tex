\chapter{Floorplanning: Problem and Related Work}
\label{sec:problem}

This chapter defines the problem, summarises its complexity, reviews the representations of floorplans used by optimisation algorithms and the optimisation methods applied to floorplanning, and describes the benchmarks used in this thesis.

\section{Problem Formulation}

An instance consists of $n$ modules $M = \{1, \dots, n\}$; module $i$ is a rectangle of width $w_i$ and height $h_i$ (positive integers). A {\em placement} assigns to every module a position $(x_i, y_i)$ of its lower left corner and an orientation $r_i \in \{0, 1\}$: a rotated module ($r_i = 1$) has its dimensions swapped. Let $(w'_i, h'_i)$ denote the dimensions after rotation. A placement is {\em feasible} if no two modules overlap:
\begin{equation}
\forall i \neq j: \quad x_i + w'_i \leq x_j \;\vee\; x_j + w'_j \leq x_i \;\vee\; y_i + h'_i \leq y_j \;\vee\; y_j + h'_j \leq y_i .
\end{equation}
With all coordinates non-negative, the enclosing rectangle has the width $W = \max_i (x_i + w'_i)$ and the height $H = \max_i (y_i + h'_i)$, and the task is to find a feasible placement with the minimal area $A = W \cdot H$. The quality of a placement is reported as the {\em dead space}
\begin{equation}
d = \frac{W \cdot H}{\sum_{i \in M} w_i h_i} - 1 ,
\end{equation}
the unused area relative to the area of the modules, which is the measure used in the original edition and in much of the floorplanning literature. A placement with $d = 0$ uses the enclosing rectangle perfectly; the optimal dead space of a given instance is usually unknown and may be greater than zero. Fig.~\ref{fig:problem} shows an example.

\caption{An example instance and a feasible placement of its modules}

In practical floorplanning, the area is usually combined with the total wire length of the nets connecting the modules, and modern floorplanners often place the modules into a fixed outline given in advance instead of minimising the area \cite{adya}. The thesis studies the classical area minimisation of hard (fixed-shape) rotatable modules, which is the setting of the original edition and of many papers on floorplan representations \cite{btree,otree,sp}.

\section{Complexity}

The decision version of the problem, whether the modules fit into a rectangle of given width and height, is NP-complete in the strong sense: if all modules have height one and widths greater than $k$, so that none of them can be rotated, and the rectangle has height $k$, the modules have to be packed into $k$ rows, which is exactly the strongly NP-complete bin packing problem with $k$ bins \cite{garey}. The problem remains strongly NP-complete when all modules are squares \cite{leung}, and related packing problems in the plane were shown to be NP-complete already by Fowler et al.~\cite{fowler}. Consequently, exact methods are limited to small instances and floorplanners rely on heuristics and metaheuristics, which search over an encoding of the floorplans, the {\em representation}.

\section{Floorplan Representations}

A representation encodes a set of floorplans by combinatorial objects. Good representations can encode an optimal floorplan, can be decoded quickly into a placement, and allow simple modifications (moves) for local search or evolutionary algorithms. Floorplans are divided into {\em slicing} floorplans, which can be obtained by recursively cutting the enclosing rectangle into two by horizontal or vertical lines, and general {\em non-slicing} floorplans. Table~\ref{tab:representations} summarises the representations discussed below.

\subsection{Slicing Trees}

A slicing floorplan is represented by a binary tree whose leaves are the modules and whose internal nodes are the operators of a vertical or a horizontal join of the two sub-floorplans. Written in post-order, the tree becomes a Polish expression, the representation of the classical simulated annealing floorplanner of Wong and Liu \cite{pe}. Slicing floorplans are easy to decode, but they cannot represent every floorplan directly; Lai and Wong \cite{pe2} showed that, combined with compaction, slicing trees can generate all maximally compact placements. \subsection{Sequence Pairs}

A sequence pair \cite{sp} is a pair of permutations of the modules. The relative order of two modules in both sequences determines their geometric relation: if $a$ precedes $b$ in both sequences, $a$ lies to the left of $b$; if $a$ precedes $b$ in the first sequence and follows it in the second, $a$ lies above $b$. Every sequence pair corresponds to a feasible placement, which is obtained by computing the longest paths in the horizontal and vertical constraint graphs. The representation can encode every floorplan, so its solution space always contains an optimal one. An example is given in Fig.~\ref{fig:sp}. Related graph-based representations include the bounded slicing grid \cite{bsg}, the corner block list \cite{cbl} and the transitive closure graph \cite{tcg,tcgs}.

\subsection{O-Trees and B*-Trees}

A placement is {\em L-compact} ({\em B-compact}) if no module can be moved to the left (down) without overlapping another module, and {\em admissible} if it is both. Every placement can be transformed into an admissible placement that is not larger by alternating compactions to the left and down, so it suffices to search among admissible placements. The O-Tree \cite{otree} represents admissible placements by ordered trees: a module is placed next to its parent and as low as the already placed modules allow. The B*-Tree \cite{btree} encodes the same information by an ordered binary tree. The root module is placed at the origin; the left child of a module is placed immediately to its right, and the right child immediately above it at the same $x$ coordinate. The $y$ coordinate of every module is the lowest one at which it does not overlap the modules placed before it in the depth-first (pre-order) traversal of the tree. Fig.~\ref{fig:otree} and Fig.~\ref{fig:btree} give examples.

The placement of a B*-Tree is computed with a {\em contour}, the upper envelope of the modules placed so far, kept as a list of horizontal segments. The $y$ coordinate of a new module is the maximum height of the contour over its horizontal extent, after which the contour is raised to its top (Fig.~\ref{fig:contour}). With the contour, the placement of the whole tree takes linear time \cite{otree,btree} (Section~\ref{sec:placement}). The B*-Tree supports simple moves (rotation of a module, swap of two modules, removal and insertion of a node) and extensions to pre-placed, soft and rectilinear modules \cite{btree,btreesa}, which made it popular in academic floorplanners and the representation of choice in this thesis.

\section{Optimisation Methods}

\subsection{Simulated Annealing}

Simulated annealing \cite{sa} is the most common optimisation method in floorplanning. Starting from a random solution, it repeatedly applies a random move and accepts the result if it is not worse or, otherwise, with the probability $e^{-\Delta/T}$, where $\Delta$ is the deterioration and the temperature $T$ decreases during the run. It was used with Polish expressions \cite{pe}, sequence pairs \cite{sp}, O-Trees \cite{otree} and B*-Trees \cite{btree}; the {\em Fast-SA} of Chen and Chang \cite{btreesa} uses a three-stage temperature schedule and B*-Trees and also addresses fixed-outline floorplanning. Late acceptance hill climbing \cite{lahc} is a more recent alternative with a single parameter: a candidate is accepted if it is not worse than the current solution or than the solution $L$ steps ago.

\subsection{Evolutionary Algorithms}

Evolutionary algorithms \cite{ga,eiben} maintain a population of candidate solutions that are selected according to their quality (fitness) and recombined and mutated to create new candidates. Their application to floorplanning is complicated by the representations: a crossover of two trees or permutations usually needs to be repaired or specially designed. Memetic algorithms combine the evolutionary search with local search; Tang and Yao \cite{memetic} report optimal or near optimal results on popular benchmarks with a hybrid of a genetic algorithm and local search for non-slicing floorplans of hard modules.

\subsection{POEMS}

POEMS \cite{poems} avoids recombining whole solutions. It works with a single solution, the {\em prototype}, which is improved in iterations. In each iteration, an evolutionary algorithm searches the space of short sequences of {\em actions}, parameterised modifications of the prototype; the fitness of a sequence is the quality of the prototype modified by it. The best sequence found in the iteration is applied to the prototype if the result is not worse. POEMS was introduced on binary string optimisation and the travelling salesman problem \cite{poems}. The author's bachelor thesis \cite{vh} applied it to floorplanning with slicing trees and the original edition of this thesis to B*-Trees.
