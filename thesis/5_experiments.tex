This chapter answers the research questions of Chapter~\ref{sec:introduction}. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the methodology common to all experiments. Section~\ref{sec:rq1} measures the effect of the implementation (RQ1). \section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Budget.} Every run has a fixed wall-clock time limit, measured from the start of the optimisation, after the benchmark has been loaded; it includes the just-in-time compilation of the program, which takes place during the run. Equal time, rather than an equal number of evaluations, is the fair basis for comparing implementations and algorithms whose steps have different costs. The results of the original edition were reported after a fixed number of iterations, so their running times are given with them.

\paragraph{Seeds.} Every run has its own seed of the random number generator. The screening experiments (Sections~\ref{sec:rq1}--\ref{sec:rq3}) use the seeds 1--8 or 1--6. The final comparison (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}) uses the fresh seeds 101--108 on the tuning benchmarks and 101--105 on the held-out benchmarks; these seeds were not used when the configurations were chosen.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.} The eight benchmarks of Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} were used for development and tuning; apte is omitted from most screening tables because every variant finds the same floorplan (0.78\,\%). The six held-out GSRC instances were used only in the final comparison.

\paragraph{Measures.} The quality of a run is the dead space of its best floorplan (Chapter~\ref{sec:problem}). The tables report the mean and the population standard deviation over the seeds, and the best run in parentheses. Every floorplan was validated independently (Chapter~\ref{sec:implementation}).

\paragraph{Statistics.} Two variants are compared by the two-sided Mann-Whitney U test \cite{mannwhitney} at the level 0.05 on the per-seed results. In the tables, a {\bf bold} entry is significantly better and an entry marked ${}^\blacktriangle$ significantly worse than the first (reference) column of the table, unless the caption says otherwise. The final comparison also reports the Vargha-Delaney effect size $\hat{A}_{12}$ \cite{vargha}, the probability that a run of one algorithm ends with less dead space than a run of the other (ties count one half). To compare configurations across benchmarks, the {\em score} of a configuration is the geometric mean over the benchmarks of its mean dead space divided by the best mean dead space among the compared configurations; the best possible score is 1.

\paragraph{Platform.} All experiments ran on a virtual machine with four cores of an Intel Xeon at 2.8\,GHz and OpenJDK~21, with the serial garbage collector and a heap of at most 1\,GB. Three runs ran in parallel, so that every run had a core of its own and the fourth core was left for the system; runs that are compared with each other always ran under the same conditions. The machine is about 1.7 times faster than the 2\,GHz machine of the original edition: the original program needed 131.7\,s for 1000 iterations on n100 there (Table~5.3 of the original edition) and needs 77\,s here (24\,000 evaluations per second, mean of 8 runs).

\section{Threats to Validity}

\paragraph{Tuning on the same benchmarks.} The configurations of the improved POEMS and of LA-POEMS were chosen on the same eight benchmarks on which they are compared. Two measures reduce the resulting optimistic bias: the final comparison uses seeds that were not used for tuning, and the held-out instances were not used at all before the final comparison.

\paragraph{Implementation and algorithm.} The original POEMS runs in its original, slower implementation, so its comparison with the two new algorithms mixes the effects of the implementation and of the algorithm. The improved POEMS and LA-POEMS share the implementation; their comparison, and that of Table~\ref{tab:rq3equal}, isolates the algorithm.

\paragraph{Time measurement.} The runs share a machine with other runs, so their speed varies by a few percent; runs that are compared were always started in the same configuration of parallel runs. The time limits include the just-in-time compilation, which matters only for the shortest runs.

\paragraph{Sample size and multiple comparisons.} Five to eight seeds per configuration detect only large differences, and non-significant differences in the screening tables should not be read as evidence of equal performance. Conversely, the screening tables contain several hundred tests without a correction for multiple comparisons; at the level 0.05, about one test in twenty is significant by chance, so a single significant cell is weak evidence. The conclusions of this chapter therefore rely on consistent patterns across benchmarks and on the final comparison with new seeds.

\paragraph{Comparison with the literature.} The results of other algorithms are taken from their publications, which used other machines, other time budgets and in some cases other definitions of the measured quantity. They indicate the order of magnitude of the results; they are not a controlled comparison.
