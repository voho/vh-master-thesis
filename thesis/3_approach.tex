\chapter{Methods}
\label{sec:approach}

This chapter describes how a B*-Tree is turned into a placement, how the initial solution is constructed, the POEMS algorithm for B*-Trees from the original edition, an analysis of the behaviour of its genetic algorithm, the improvements of POEMS evaluated in Chapter~\ref{sec:testing}, and the new late acceptance POEMS.

\section{Placement of a B*-Tree}
\label{sec:placement}

The modules are placed in the depth-first pre-order of the tree (a node, then its left subtree, then its right subtree). The root is placed at $x = 0$; the left child of a node $p$ at $x = x_p + w'_p$ and the right child at $x = x_p$. The $y$ coordinate of a module is the lowest one at which it does not overlap any module placed before it, that is, the maximum of $y_j + h'_j$ over the placed modules $j$ whose horizontal extent overlaps the interval $[x, x + w')$, or zero if there is none.

\subsection{The Contour}

The {\em contour} is the upper envelope of the placed modules: a list of horizontal segments $[a_k, b_k)$ ordered from the left, covering $[0, \infty)$, each with the height (level) of the highest module above it. To place a module at $x$, the segments overlapping $[x, x + w')$ are walked and the maximal level is its $y$ coordinate; then the walked segments are replaced by a single segment $[x, x + w')$ at the level $y + h'$, keeping the parts of the first and the last segment that are not covered. Since the new level is above every replaced level, the list stays the upper envelope, and the maximal level over an interval is the maximal top of the modules overlapping it.

In the pre-order traversal, the segment where a module starts is known without searching. The left child of $p$ starts at $x_p + w'_p$, which is the start of the segment following the segment of $p$; the right child starts at $x_p$, the start of the segment of $p$. The segment of $p$ is not changed before its right child is placed, because all modules of the left subtree of $p$ lie to the right of $x_p + w'_p$. Hence no search is needed, every placement creates at most one new segment (the uncovered rest of the last walked segment), and every walked segment except the last one is removed. The placement of the whole tree therefore takes $O(n)$ time, as stated in \cite{otree,btree}.

The original implementation kept all placed modules in a priority queue ordered by their tops and walked it until the first module overlapping the new one horizontally. This gives the same coordinates but takes $O(n)$ time per module in the worst case and $O(n^2)$ per placement; Section~\ref{sec:rq1} compares both.

\subsection{Early Termination}
\label{sec:earlyexit}

During the placement, the width and the height of the enclosing rectangle of the modules placed so far never decrease. If a search algorithm only needs to know whether the area of a candidate is at most some threshold $\theta$ (for example, the area that would still be accepted), the placement can stop as soon as $W \cdot H > \theta$: the candidate would be rejected anyway. This does not change any decision of the algorithm, only saves time on the rejected candidates.

\section{The Prototype}

POEMS starts from an initial B*-Tree, the prototype. The original edition proposed two constructions; the {\em best-fit} construction was used in all experiments. First, every module is rotated to be at least as wide as high, and the modules are sorted by width (descending) and then by height (descending). The floorplan is then filled shelf by shelf. The width of a shelf is the larger of the widest module and the square root of the total area of the modules. The next module placed on the current shelf is the first module in the sorted order that fits into its remaining width. If no module fits, a new shelf is started. In the tree, a module placed next to the previous one becomes its left child, and the first module of a new shelf becomes the right child of the first module of the previous shelf. The alternative {\em complete tree} construction stores the sorted modules in a complete binary tree in the order of an array-based heap. Fig.~\ref{fig:prototype} shows both.

The original implementation of the best-fit construction iterated over a priority queue, which in Java does not iterate in the priority order, so it did not pick the widest fitting module as described. Corrected, it produces prototypes with somewhat less dead space on four of the eight benchmarks (for example 17.0\,\% instead of 18.5\,\% on n300 and 20.8\,\% instead of 22.7\,\% on xerox) and the same prototypes on the others.

\section{POEMS for B*-Trees}

\subsection{Actions}

The nodes of the prototype are numbered $0, \dots, n-1$ in pre-order. A number identifies a module: when an action moves a module, its number moves with it. An action has an {\em enabled} flag and parameters (numbers of nodes and flags); a disabled action does nothing. Table~\ref{tab:actions} lists the seven actions. The first six come from the original edition; the {\em move node} action was added in this revision. It is the standard B*-Tree move of a single node \cite{btree}, finer than moving whole subtrees by the hang node action.

\subsection{Action Sequences and Niches}

An individual of the genetic algorithm is a sequence of $N$ actions applied to the prototype one after another. The number of its enabled actions is its {\em niche}. If the enabled and the disabled actions were treated equally, the population would soon consist of sequences with few enabled actions, as most modifications of a good prototype make it worse. The population is therefore divided into $N$ niches of $S$ individuals each; niche $k$ contains only sequences with at least $k$ enabled actions.

The fitness of a sequence is the negative unused area $-(W \cdot H - \sum_i w_i h_i)$ of the modified prototype. A sequence that does not change the prototype, for example two flips of the same module, gets the minimal fitness, so that the algorithm searches for real changes. The original implementation compared the trees by the node numbers only. A sequence that just rotated modules was therefore considered unchanged and rejected, so rotations were only ever applied together with structural changes. The comparison now includes the orientations of the modules.

\subsection{The Genetic Algorithm}

Each iteration runs a steady-state genetic algorithm for $G$ generations on a new random population (Fig.~\ref{fig:poems}). In a generation, a niche is chosen at random and parents are selected from it by a binary tournament in which the better individual wins with the probability 0.95 \cite{tournament}. With the probability 0.7, two parents are recombined by the uniform crossover of their action sequences; otherwise one parent is mutated. The mutation either changes the order of the actions (probability 0.75) or replaces each action with the probability 0.4 by a variant of the same type with possibly changed flags and parameters. A new individual replaces the first individual in the population (scanning from the first niche to its own) that has a worse or equal fitness; if there is none, it is discarded. A sequence without enabled actions is replaced by a random one. The best sequence of the iteration is applied to the prototype if the result is not worse than the prototype. The original edition used $G = 1000$, $N = 3$ and $S = 50$.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \STATE{$\beta \leftarrow$ the best-fit prototype; $\beta^* \leftarrow \beta$}
  \WHILE{time and iterations remain}
    \STATE{create a population of $N \cdot S$ random action sequences (niche $k$: $k$ enabled actions)}
    \FOR{$g = 1, \dots, G$}
      \STATE{choose a niche; select parents from it by binary tournaments}
      \STATE{create one or two children by mutation or uniform crossover and evaluate them on $\beta$}
      \STATE{insert each child in place of the first individual that is not better}
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE{$\sigma \leftarrow$ the best sequence of the iteration}
    \IF{$f(\sigma(\beta)) \geq f(\beta)$}
      \STATE{$\beta \leftarrow \sigma(\beta)$}
    \ENDIF
    \IF{$f(\beta) \geq f(\beta^*)$}
      \STATE{$\beta^* \leftarrow \beta$}
    \ENDIF
  \ENDWHILE
  \RETURN{$\beta^*$}
\end{algorithmic}

\end{figure}

\section{What the Genetic Algorithm Does}
\label{sec:gaanalysis}

To see how POEMS spends its evaluations, the genetic algorithm was instrumented. Every child was compared with its parents and with the individuals in the population, and for every iteration the generation of the last improvement of the best individual and the outcome of the iteration were recorded. Table~\ref{tab:gaanalysis} summarises 20 second runs of the corrected algorithm with the parameters of the original edition.

\input{tables/gaanalysis}

The table shows three things. First, almost half of the children (45--47\,\% on every benchmark) are copies of a sequence that is already in the population, mostly mutations that changed nothing and crossovers of equal parents; their evaluation brings no new information. Second, in at least half of the iterations the best sequence is already present in the initial random population (the median generation of the last improvement is 0 on seven of the eight benchmarks), so most of the 1000 generations of an iteration cannot change the step that is finally applied. Third, most iterations end with a step of equal fitness, a move on a plateau of the objective (96--100\,\% of the iterations on seven benchmarks, 67\,\% on ami49); only 0--3.4\,\% of them improve the prototype, which costs 54\,000 to 4 million evaluations per improvement. Meanwhile a single random action improves the prototype much more often (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}). These observations motivate both the improvements of POEMS below and the new algorithm.

\section{Improvements of POEMS}
\label{sec:improvements}

The following modifications are optional switches of the implementation (Appendix~\ref{sec:usage}); Section~\ref{sec:rq2} evaluates each of them separately, together with smaller numbers of generations per iteration.

\begin{description}
\item[Array evaluation.] Each action can also be applied in place to an array representation of the B*-Tree (Section~\ref{sec:arraybtree}). The prototype is converted once per iteration and every sequence is evaluated on a copy of its arrays; the identity check compares the trees exactly. The results are identical to the original evaluation, only faster.
\item[Duplicate elimination.] Each sequence has a 64-bit signature computed from its enabled actions (with the parameters of the symmetric exchange actions ordered). A child whose sequence is already in the population is discarded without being evaluated.
\item[Early termination.] An iteration ends after $P$ generations without an improvement of the best individual ({\em patience}), or as soon as a sequence better than the prototype is found ({\em first improvement}).
\item[Late acceptance of prototypes.] The result of an iteration is accepted if it is not worse than the prototype or than the prototype $L$ iterations ago \cite{lahc}, which allows the search to leave local optima.
\item[Move node action.] The seventh action of Table~\ref{tab:actions}.
\end{description}

\section{Late Acceptance POEMS}
\label{sec:lapoems}

The analysis of Section~\ref{sec:gaanalysis} suggests keeping what POEMS does well, improving a single prototype by short sequences of actions, and changing the search for the steps. Late acceptance POEMS (LA-POEMS, Fig.~\ref{fig:lapoems}) applies a random action sequence to the prototype in every step and decides immediately whether the modified prototype replaces it. The niches of POEMS correspond to the lengths of the steps: a step consists of $k$ enabled actions, with $k$ chosen uniformly from $1, \dots, N$. The types and the parameters of the actions are chosen uniformly at random.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \STATE{$\beta \leftarrow$ the best-fit prototype; $\beta^* \leftarrow \beta$; $\mathit{history}[0..L-1] \leftarrow A(\beta)$}
  \FOR{$t = 0, 1, \dots$ while time remains}
    \STATE{$\gamma \leftarrow$ a copy of $\beta$; apply $k$ random actions to $\gamma$, $k$ uniform in $1..N$}
    \STATE{$\theta \leftarrow \max(A(\beta), \mathit{history}[t \bmod L])$ \COMMENT{late acceptance}}
    \STATE{\quad or $\theta \leftarrow A(\beta) - T(t) \ln u$, $u \sim U(0, 1]$ \COMMENT{simulated annealing}}
    \IF{$A(\gamma) \leq \theta$ (the placement stops as soon as the area exceeds $\theta$)}
      \STATE{$\beta \leftarrow \gamma$; if $A(\beta) < A(\beta^*)$ then $\beta^* \leftarrow \beta$}
    \ENDIF
    \STATE{$\mathit{history}[t \bmod L] \leftarrow A(\beta)$}
  \ENDFOR
  \RETURN{$\beta^*$}
\end{algorithmic}
\caption{Late acceptance POEMS (with the two acceptance criteria)}
\label{fig:lapoems}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Acceptance}

Two acceptance criteria are compared. {\em Late acceptance hill climbing} \cite{lahc} accepts the candidate if its area is not greater than the area of the current prototype or of the prototype $L$ steps ago. Because the best history length depends on the number of steps that can be made in the given time, the implementation can also choose it automatically. It measures the evaluation speed during the first 2\,\% of the time and sets $L$ to the expected number of steps divided by a constant $C$. {\em Simulated annealing} \cite{sa} accepts a deterioration $\Delta$ with the probability $e^{-\Delta/T}$. The temperature decreases exponentially from $T_0$ to $T_1$ over the time budget, $T(t) = T_0 (T_1/T_0)^{t/t_{\max}}$, with both temperatures given relative to the area of the modules. Both criteria are written as a threshold on the area of the candidate, which is known before the candidate is placed. This allows the early termination of the placement (Section~\ref{sec:earlyexit}).

\subsection{Further Options}

Four further options were evaluated (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}). With {\em weights}, the action types are chosen with given relative probabilities instead of uniformly. With {\em restarts}, the search restarts from the initial prototype (keeping the best solution) when the best solution of the current run has not improved for $R \cdot n$ steps. With {\em local partners}, the second module of a two-module action is, with a given probability, chosen by a random walk of one to three steps from the first module in the tree, which makes the step more local. With {\em adaptive action selection}, the action types are chosen in proportion to their success rates (the fraction of steps using the type that improved the prototype) in the last window of $10^5$ steps, with a minimal probability of 5\,\% for each type.

The configuration chosen by the experiments of Section~\ref{sec:rq3}, which is the default of the program, uses simulated annealing with $T_0 = 10^{-2}$ and $T_1 = 10^{-5}$, steps of at most $N = 2$ actions and local partners with the probability 0.5.

\subsection{Relation to POEMS}

LA-POEMS keeps the representation, the actions, the prototype and the idea of improving it by steps of several actions, but replaces the evolution of the steps by sampling and a simple acceptance criterion. One step costs one evaluation instead of one iteration of about 1850 evaluations, and the prototype changes as soon as an acceptable step is found. The price is that steps are not refined: a step that is almost good is not recombined with other steps. Chapter~\ref{sec:testing} examines whether this price is worth paying.
